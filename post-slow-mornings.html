On the discipline of slow mornings

September 2026 · 6 min read

Most productivity advice treats the morning as a machine to optimize: wake at a fixed hour, drink water before coffee, journal for exactly ten minutes, exercise before the willpower runs out. I've tried versions of this system for years, and it always works for about nine days before it starts to feel like something being done to me rather than something I'm doing.

What's changed recently isn't the routine itself, but what I think a morning is for. I've stopped treating it as the first lap of the day's race and started treating it as a room I get to sit in before the race starts. Nobody is keeping score in that room. The point isn't to extract value from the first hour — it's to be a person for a while before becoming useful.

This sounds precious written down, so let me be specific about what it actually looks like. I still wake up early, but I no longer open my phone first. I make tea in a pot instead of a cup, which takes four minutes longer and forces four minutes of standing still. I sit by the window with nothing to do, which is a sentence that would have embarrassed me two years ago.

The strange result is that I get more done, not less — but that was never really the point, and I try not to let it become the point again. The morning is the one part of the day I still fully own. Everything after it belongs, in some fraction, to other people's schedules and other people's needs. I'd rather protect one slow hour than optimize twenty-three fast ones.
